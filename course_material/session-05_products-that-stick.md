## 2. Part 1 — Habit

*They come back without thinking about it.* (25 min)

### A habit is a product you open without choosing
A feature is shipped; a habit returns the rest of the week. The difference is whether the user's day has a slot for you — not whether your launch was loud.

| A feature | A habit |
|---|---|
| Opened once | Opened on almost all days |
| Sits on a release page, maybe gets shared, mostly forgotten in three weeks | Without being asked, without thinking — the product has rented a slot in their day, week after week |
| Used once · no slot in their day · campaign-driven | Frequent · cue-driven · low-effort |

> Products do not become habits by being good enough. They become habits when the user's day is shaped around them.

### The two axes that separate them — frequency × effort

| | **Low effort** | **Higher effort** |
|---|---|---|
| **High frequency** | **Habit territory** — runs without thinking; no decision to open it | **Occasional task** — used when needed (tax filing, payroll). A chore, not a habit |
| **Low frequency** | **Recognisable form** — not used often but present in the mind; visible in their hand | **Dormant** — used when forced (tax, audit), once a quarter. Shipped, forgotten, decay |

The green corner (habit territory) is the hardest to reach — and pays the most.

### The Hook Model (Nir Eyal, 2014)
*A four-step loop that makes them a routine.* Cue → action → reward → investment → the next cue.

#### 01 · Trigger — the cue that brings them back
| External triggers | Internal triggers |
|---|---|
| The world nudges them: notification, a friend's story, an email subject line. Free to send, costly to ignore. | They nudge themselves: mood, time of day, a recurring thought. Free to send, impossible to fake. |

Examples with real cost: Swiggy's "Your order is on the way"; LinkedIn's weekly digest; Father's Day sale push. External triggers fade with notification fatigue. **Internal triggers — identity, emotion, time — are the ones that scale.** Most products obsess over external triggers because they're measurable; the internal ones *are* the product in the long run.

#### 02 · Action — the cheapest path you can offer
**Behaviour = Motivation × Ability × Prompt** (Fogg)
- **Make it easy** — cut the steps. One tap beats two. Predict beats fast.
- **Find the right motive** — want, not should. The user's why, not yours.
- **Make it obvious** — show the next thing. The cue should anticipate the moment, not arrive after.

The action step is the discipline. Motivation comes and goes — friction stays.

#### 03 · Variable reward — pleasure with a question mark
A predictable reward trains; an unpredictable one compels. *The brain of a habit-former is a slot machine, not a vending machine.*

| The Tribe — social reward | The Hunt — reward of information | The Bones — reward of mastery |
|---|---|---|
| Likes, replies, retweets, "X liked this" — being seen. LinkedIn celebrates a reaction; Instagram rolls a new count. | Search reveals something we didn't know a moment ago. Google's snippet, the Swiggy tracker, the CRED statement that opens. | A streak you maintain, a level you finish, a word you finally remembered. Duolingo's 1,000-day mark, Pokémon GO's Evolve button. |

Build for whichever fits your user — one at a time, not all three.

#### 04 · Investment — the user does the work, not the product
Each visit loads the next trigger. The user stores value in the product, so leaving costs more than staying.

| Type | What's stored | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **Data** | Content, history, profile | The user's own work loaded into the product; switching becomes export-shaped |
| **Followers** | Audience accumulated (Instagram, Twitter, Substack, LinkedIn) | A following is a moat |
| **Credits** | Skill, streak, level | The user improves and doesn't want to lose it — sunk cost on the user's side |
| **Content loaded into** | Tools plugged in — bookings, designs, repositories | The workspace is theirs |

The loop restarts because the user unknowingly paid for the next visit.

### Frequency × intensity — build for the shape
Two habits live in the same week: one shallow and constant, one deep and rare.

| Shallow & constant — daily ritual | Deep & rare — anchored moment |
|---|---|
| Low stakes per visit, many visits; light enough to run without thinking (three times a week, a single minute) | Higher stakes per visit, fewer visits; the week has a slot for it (once a month, sized for a Sunday) |

Duolingo is daily and small; CRED billing is monthly and big. Both can be habits; neither is better.

### Habit case studies

**01 · Duolingo — "A streak you don't want to break."**
| Trigger (external) | Action | Variable reward | Investment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Push at 8 pm — friendly reminder, "worst part of the day" | One small lesson — five minutes, predictable | XP, lingots, +1 day — three kinds at once (social, mastery, hunt) | Streak, level, friends — the user loads tomorrow's lesson |

The streak doesn't pay Duolingo — it pays the user, which makes the loop continuous. *The number that mattered was never the lesson; it was the day-count being broken.*

**02 · WhatsApp, India — "The chat app that replaced another."**
- **Old:** SMS charged by the message; group conversation and photos impossible; cost a limit on intimacy.
- **Replaced with:** free, over data, group by default. A green dot at 22:30 — the whole family across cities in one thread.
- The habit lives in three taps: the green dot, the family thread, the typing cursor. *WhatsApp is not "open the app." It is "reopen the conversation." Different verb.*

**03 · CRED, India — "A bill-paying habit that sells status."**
The number-one job is "pay my credit card" — that's not the habit.
- **Reward · social:** a score, a tile, a leaderboard — CRED coins, cashback, "high member".
- **Reward · hunt:** a surprise drop, a brand — offers that change which day you open it.
- **Investment:** credit history on file — switching to PhonePe/GPay is export-shaped.

*The bill is the excuse. The habit is being seen as creditworthy.* Same move as Duolingo — swap the "punishment" for a public score.

### Quiz — a quick read of your own product (carry back to your S4 MVP)
1. **The internal trigger:** after launch week, why would the user open the app on Day 8? (a) push they'll tap (b) an emotion or moment they recognise (c) habit of opening with no cue (d) don't have one yet.
2. **The reward:** which reward does the user get that they couldn't predict? social / hunt / mastery / more than one / don't know yet.
3. **The investment:** what does the user load into the product that would be costly to walk away from? their own data / a following / skill or streak / a workspace / none yet.

"None yet" is information, not failure.

## 3. Part 2 — Laws of UX

*It feels obvious, without anyone thinking.* (22 min)

Based on **Jon Yablonski, *Laws of UX*, 2nd ed. (O'Reilly, 2024)** — "Using psychology to design better products & services." Each law is presented with what it says, how to **apply** it, and what to **remember**.

### Familiarity & expectations
| Law | Principle | Apply | Remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Jakob's Law** | People bring expectations from products they already know (a familiar store layout transfers to yours) | Keep familiar navigation and interaction patterns | Innovate where it helps, and test unfamiliar patterns |
| **Mental models** | A person's expectation of how a system works (e.g. "I can get it back" from Trash) | Research what people expect before changing a familiar workflow | A major redesign can break learned behaviour — give people a clear transition (preview → try → transition) |

### Physical & perceptual limits
| Law | Principle | Apply | Remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Fitts's Law** | Larger, closer targets take less time to reach accurately | Make important targets easy to reach, generous in size, well spaced | A visible icon and its clickable area can differ — test the actual target |
| **Human factors** | People make mistakes when controls ignore real human limitations (identical knobs confuse; aircraft flap vs landing-gear levers are shaped differently) | Design for distraction, stress, varied dexterity, and attention | Investigate the interface before blaming an error on the user |

### Memory & cognitive load
| Law | Principle | Apply | Remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Miller's Law** | Working memory is limited (the famous 7 ± 2); meaningful chunks help retention | Organise related information into meaningful groups | Capacity varies; seven is not a universal interface limit (not a menu-item limit) |
| **Cognitive load** | Every extra thing a user must remember competes with the task (remembering an offer code vs keeping it visible at checkout) | Reduce memory demands with clear labels, visible context, useful defaults | A visually sparse screen can still require substantial mental effort |
| **Chunking** | Grouping related information makes it easier to scan (9384710265 → 938 471 0265; contact vs delivery address blocks) | Group by meaning; reinforce groups with spacing and hierarchy | Chunking organises content; it doesn't impose a fixed number of items |

### Choices & structure
| Law | Principle | Apply | Remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Hick's Law** | Decision time rises as choices become more numerous or complex (long undifferentiated menu vs organised Create / Review / Manage) | Present relevant choices, group options, make the next action clear | Keep enough context to recognise an option — fewer labels can create more confusion |
| **Choice overload** | A large assortment can make choosing harder and less satisfying (a wall of jars vs three: for beginners / frequent use / specialists) | Help people narrow options using goals and meaningful differences | More choice isn't always worse — the task and the user's knowledge matter |
| **Card sorting** | Reveals how people expect information to be grouped and named (choose topics → group → name → discuss) | Ask representative users to organise topics and explain their reasoning | Open sorts let users name groups; closed sorts test predefined categories |
| **Postel's Law** | Accept reasonable variation in input; provide clear, dependable output (98765 43210 / 98765-43210 / (98765) 43210 → normalised) | Be flexible with formatting, explain requirements, give helpful feedback | Ambiguous input needs clarification; tolerance still needs sensible boundaries |
| **Resilient, inclusive design** | A robust interface adapts to differences in people, content and devices (screen size, longer translations, text enlargement) | Plan for varied names, languages, input methods and text sizes | Test beyond the default device, language and font setting |

### Memory of experience & bias
| Law | Principle | Apply | Remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Peak-End Rule** | Emotional peaks and the ending strongly shape remembered experience (payment error → helpful recovery → clear confirmation) | Improve intense moments; make the ending clear and reassuring | A pleasant ending doesn't excuse a harmful or frustrating journey |
| **Cognitive biases** | Mental shortcuts help decide quickly but distort judgement — **confirmation bias** (favouring evidence matching a belief) and **recency effect** (final items stand out) | Look for disconfirming evidence; examine the full experience | A memorable comment or recent event may not represent the whole pattern |
| **Negativity bias** | A negative moment weighs heavily in memory | Explain requirements early (prevent confusion), preserve user effort, offer a way forward (support recovery) | Humour must fit the context; solving the problem comes first |
| **Aesthetic-Usability Effect** | People perceive attractive designs as easier to use | Use visual quality to support clarity, confidence and a positive first impression | Attractiveness can mask usability problems — watch actual task performance |
| **Automatic cognitive processing** | People switch between System 1 (fast, automatic) and System 2 (slow, effortful) | Make routine actions easy to recognise and important decisions easy to examine | A fast first impression can influence later judgements |

### Attention
| Law | Principle | Apply | Remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Von Restorff Effect** | A distinctive item among similar items is more memorable (one highlighted "Continue" among grey buttons) | Reserve visual emphasis for the most relevant information and actions | When everything stands out, important items compete for attention |
| **Selective attention** | People focus on what seems relevant to their current goal | Align hierarchy and labels with the task users are trying to complete | Something can be visible without receiving attention |
| **Banner blindness** | People ignore useful content that looks like advertising ("Service unavailable on Sunday" as a promo-style box is ignored; inline near the booking form is seen) | Put essential info where it relates to the task, and make it look like content | Extra visual emphasis can make a message look *less* relevant |
| **Change blindness** | People miss changes when attention is elsewhere (cart total changes from ₹500 to ₹1,000) | Show meaningful changes near the action with a clear status message ("Quantity updated") | Don't rely on a distant or fleeting visual change alone |
| **Accessible visual emphasis** | Contrast is useful only when people can perceive and interpret it | Combine colour with words or shape, keep text legible, make focus visible | Use motion carefully; respect people who need reduced motion |
| **Eye tracking** | Shows where people look during a task (define the question → calibrate → run the task → interpret with other evidence) | Use gaze patterns to investigate attention, navigation and overlooked content | Looking doesn't prove understanding — combine gaze data with observation and interviews |

### Complexity
| Law | Principle | Apply | Remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tesler's Law** (conservation of complexity) | Some complexity always remains — either the system or the user handles it ("Shipping address is the same" reuses billing details) | Use sensible defaults, reuse known data, automate repetitive work | Preserve visibility and control over important decisions |
| **Complexity bias** | A complicated explanation or solution can seem more credible than a simple one | Understand the underlying problem before adding features or rules | Judge a solution by how well it serves the task, not its sophistication |
| **Intent-based interaction** | Natural language lets people describe an outcome instead of learning commands ("Show monthly sales by region") | Use intent to reduce the knowledge needed to access complex capabilities | Keep assumptions and results reviewable when the system interprets a request |
| **Paradox of the active user** | People start doing a task before learning the most efficient method | Provide contextual guidance and safe opportunities to learn by doing (help at the moment of need) | Don't make success depend on reading a manual first |
| **Progressive disclosure** | Show essential options first; reveal detail when relevant (core task + "Advanced options") | Make secondary options discoverable without crowding the main task | Keep required information and important consequences visible |

### Speed, flow & waiting
| Law | Principle | Apply | Remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Doherty Threshold** | Fast feedback (≈400 ms or less) maintains attention and productive rhythm (Save → "Saving…" → "Saved") | Acknowledge actions promptly, even when completion takes longer | The 400 ms benchmark is a guide, not a guarantee for every task |
| **Flow** | Focused engagement when challenge and skill are well matched (too difficult → frustration; too easy → boredom) | Support clear goals, timely feedback, appropriate challenge | Engagement should serve the user's purpose — and allow them to stop |
| **Perceived performance** | Feedback makes a wait feel more understandable (skeleton screens, progressive image preview, progress + status) | Show what's happening; reserve space to prevent content jumping | Improve actual speed too; keep progress and time-remaining claims honest |
| **Optimistic UI** | The interface responds immediately while a likely action finishes in the background (comment appears → sending → posted / failed: retry) | Use immediate feedback for suitable actions, and provide a recovery path | A pending action must not become an unrecoverable or misleading success |
| **The psychology of waiting** | Waiting feels easier with visible useful activity and progress (ride-hailing: view pickup instructions → "driver finishing a nearby trip" → "driver arriving") — idleness aversion, operational transparency, goal gradient | Explain the process; make genuine progress toward the goal visible | Avoid empty entertainment or fabricated progress that hides a real problem |

### Engagement, persuasion & ethics
| Law | Principle | Apply | Remember |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Variable rewards** | Unpredictable rewards encourage repeated checking (no update → new message → no update → interesting post) | Recognise when a feedback pattern encourages habits beyond the user's goal | Support user control over notifications and attention |
| **Infinite loops** | Autoplay and endless feeds remove natural stopping points | Provide stopping cues ("You are caught up — Stop here / Continue") and easy control over autoplay | Time spent is not the same as value received |
| **Social affirmation** | Likes and reactions reinforce the desire for approval (post → receive approval → check again) | Design social feedback to support meaningful connection | Repeated engagement can reflect pressure or anxiety rather than benefit |
| **Personalisation** | Recommendations adapt to behaviour and reinforce the same preferences | Make recommendations useful and give people ways to influence or reset them ("Adjust interests", "Explore something different") | Personalisation can create a narrowing filter bubble |
| **Defaults** | A preselected option strongly influences what people choose (profile visibility defaulting to Private) | Choose defaults that support users' expectations and explain consequences | People may never revisit a setting, even when it affects them substantially |
| **Reciprocity** | People feel an obligation to return a gesture or favour | Make responding voluntary and keep declining easy and respectful | A platform-generated invitation can still feel like a personal obligation |
| **Constructive friction** | A deliberate pause can prevent mistakes ("Delete this project? This permanently removes its files.") | Add friction when it protects users, clarifies consequences or supports control | Remove needless effort; avoid delays that create an illusion of work |
| **Deceptive design patterns** | A design is deceptive when it pushes people into choices they didn't intend (big "Accept" + tiny "No thanks" vs equal Accept/Decline) | Make consequences clear; give refusal or exit a usable path | A higher conversion rate can conceal a worse outcome for the user |
| **Ethical outcomes** | Responsible design measures whether a product helps people achieve their goals — activity measures (time on site, return visits) vs user outcomes (task achieved, sense of control, impact on daily life) | Combine behavioural data with conversations about people's experiences | Good intentions don't erase unintended harm — reassess after launch |
| **Beyond the happy path** | Design must account for people and situations outside the ideal: slow connection (allow offline + easy retry), interrupted task (save progress), accessibility needs (accessible alternatives), unexpected harm (clear recovery and support) | Explore failure and vulnerable cases early, with diverse perspectives; include varied people in research and decisions | Edge cases become real people's everyday experience at scale |

## 4. Part 3 — Product-led growth (PLG)

*The product sells itself — once you let it.* (35 min)

### Definition — the product does the selling
Sign up on a Tuesday, send an invite on Wednesday, hit a paid tier on Thursday — without a sales call, without the marketing team getting in the way.

**How the loop works:**
1. **Sign up — free to enter.** No card, no demo, no sales call. Email or Google.
2. **Aha — hits value.** In minutes, not weeks. The thing the product does is shown.
3. **Habit — used twice.** The hook loop kicks in; they come back a second and third time.
4. **Share — invites a friend.** A second user enters; the loop forks.

*Marketing didn't recruit the new user — the old one did.*

### Acquisition is the output, not the input
Sales-led teams buy ads and pray; PLG products build the loop and run it.
- **Paid companies:** pay for each user — CAC goes up.
- **Product-led:** each user pays for the next — CAC goes down.
- Loop: 01 Acquisition (a user enters) → 02 Activation (hits value fast) → 03 Retention (comes back) → 04 Referral (brings the next) → loop again at negative CAC.
- **What it costs:** server + engineering for the free tier — less than one ad campaign per month. **What it earns:** time spent in product, which becomes the loudest ad.

### Three growth models — an honest comparison

| | Sales-led | Marketing-led | Product-led |
|---|---|---|---|
| How | A team closes each deal. Long cycles, calls, demos, follow-ups, six-figure contracts, quarterly targets | Ads convert awareness to sign-up. Performance ads + content + SEO + lifecycle emails; demand-gen funnels | The product acquires users. Free tier, in-product sharing, viral loops; users come and bring the next |
| Best for | Six-figure enterprise — Workday, Salesforce, SAP | Consumer + SMB — HubSpot, Zoho, Canva | PLG-shaped markets — Figma, Slack, Calendly |
| CAC | High, paid in salary | Per click, measurable | The user pays for the next |
| Trap | The product is whatever the sales team can sell | Click-cost rises until unit economics break | Without a paying tier, you're running a charity |

Most real teams mix all three — PLG is rarely the whole answer, sometimes the dominant one.

### Free tier — free is not the promo, free is the front door
- **Figma:** Starter — free (3 files, solo editors; real product, real usage) → Professional — $15 per editor/month (unlimited files, components, teams — the moment collaboration is needed) → Enterprise — custom (org-wide libraries, SSO, audit; not the headline revenue driver).
- **Postman (India, observed):** Entry — free forever (free up to a usage level) → Pro — $14/user/month (pay when the team grows) → Enterprise — per seat, billed.

The free tier is not the goal; it's the ad. It runs forever; the paid tier turns on when collaboration or scale becomes painful. Users migrate upward because the cost of staying free becomes the cost of slowing the team.

### Viral loop — if k > 1, the curve bends up
**k = i × c** — *i* = invites per user, *c* = conversion. The product decides both.

| Product | Mechanism | k |
|---|---|---|
| WhatsApp | Address-book / phone-number import — frictionless invite to every existing contact | ≈ 1.5 (industry-quoted for the 2009–2012 address-book era; no peer-reviewed figure — illustrative) |
| Slack | A workspace is a team; repeats as a habit-shaped loop | Cited k varies, no consensus — measure by cohort (cf. Trusov, Bucklin & Pauwels 2008 on word-of-mouth) |
| Postman | Useful solo by default; lifts only when a collection is shared | No published k; treat as < 1 in solo use; share moments lift it qualitatively |

k just below 1 rises and decays; just above 1 curves up; well above 1 doubles in weeks.

### In-product sharing — the share button is not the loop; *output* is
The user produces something shareable as a side effect of the work.

| Pattern | Mechanism | Examples | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| **01 Public link** | Every artefact has a URL anyone can open | Notion pages, Figma files, Google Docs, Loom recordings, GitHub gists, Calendly slots | The link is the marketing; each viewer is a discoverable prospect |
| **02 Co-author** | The work can't be done alone | Figma frames with cursors, Google Docs comments, GitHub PRs, Linear cycles | Each collaborator becomes a user — the product recruits by being necessary |
| **03 Embedded** | The artefact travels with the user | Calendly widget in signatures, Loom in emails, YouTube embeds, Typeform in Notion | Distribution rides on every outbound the user writes |
| **04 Hand-off** | The work ends with the next user taking over | Razorpay pages, Meesho catalogues, Zoho invoices sent to a customer | The recipient never opted in — the sender chose for them |

A team that shares is one user away from recruiting the next.

### Time to value — minutes, ideally single digits
From sign-up to the moment the user realises what the product does. Observed anchors: **Calendly ~45 s · Loom ~60 s · Figma ~2 min · CRED ~5 min · Notion ~10 min.**
- Under 5 minutes: **82%** retained after a year. Over 30 days: **42%** — the same product, delivered differently. *(Composite of ChartMogul, ProfitWell and operator-reported 2025–2026 cohort data.)*
- Each extra hour between sign-up and value is a percentage off the loop. **If "aha" lives outside the first 15 minutes, retrofit before launch.**

### PLG in practice — six global loops
| Company | Loop |
|---|---|
| **Slack** — teams, not users | A new admin adds ten seats; every iteration of a workplace becomes ten users at zero growth cost |
| **Calendly** — embed into every email | Users paste booking links into bios, email signatures, sales decks — the link is a billboard that converts every reply |
| **Loom** — share inside the artefact | Every recording ships with a thumbnail, emoji tag and "Made with Loom" — marketing runs inside the product |
| **Notion** — templates that teach | Public pages get copied, modified and shared; Notion templates are a search term |
| **Figma** — public URLs by default | Designers share files in job applications; recruiters view them; designers get hired and join with login in hand |
| **Dropbox** — refer-a-friend, paid | Free storage for both sides (500 MB per accepted invite) — one of the first true PLG engines; helped quadruple the user base |

### PLG in India — six Indian loops
| Company | Loop |
|---|---|
| **Zoho** — fifty apps, one identity | The user adopts one app, then ten. Mail invites you to CRM; CRM invites you to Books. SSO does the cross-sell without sales calls |
| **Freshworks** — self-serve trial, no sales | Freshdesk, Freshsales, Freshmarketer have free tiers; customers pay at usage or seat limits — SMBs that "try, then buy" |
| **Postman** — public collections | Fifty million developers, Bangalore-built. Free for individuals and small teams; public API collections become the marketing |
| **Razorpay** — plug-in, not payment gateway | Buttons, links, QR — a payment button on a small business site; once one merchant is paid, they hand the link to a friend with the same need |
| **CRED** — pay a bill, flex a score | Status-driven habit; visible scores trigger referrals; the waitlist itself was a growth engine for years |
| **Meesho** — reseller shares a catalogue | A reseller forwards one catalogue link to a hundred WhatsApp groups; each click is a candidate user, each order a recruited buyer |

### PLG is not free of cost — five traps

| # | Trap | Problem | Fix |
|---|---|---|---|
| 01 | **Abuse** — free multi-accounts | Power users build ten "starter" workspaces; real customers can't be told apart | Phone + email as identity, not email alone |
| 02 | **Support burden** — help a million unpaid | Free-tier traffic fills the support inbox; free isn't lower cost, it's heavier cost in a different shape | Bots, docs, in-product recovery |
| 03 | **Value in plain sight** — why pay for free? | If every feature is free, paid is just "nice to have"; pricing has nothing to anchor against | Limits on what free *does*, not what it costs |
| 04 | **Hidden price** — paywall breaks the loop | A surprise pricing screen turns a happy user into a churn | Pricing visible from day one |
| 05 | **Not used here** — the buyer isn't the user | Enterprise IT buys while engineers use; the loop stalls at procurement | PLG only on the buyer-equals-user side |

PLG is honest about its limits: where it works, it works; where it doesn't, another model does.

### How to "product manage" the most unique product
**I am talking about you.**

**What does ~~business~~ education focus on?** An analysis of the ISB Hyderabad PGP 2026–27 course line-up, sorted by who each course teaches you about:

Four of the six "self" courses run in February–March 2027, after the likely placement window.

### Do you love yourself? — Pillars of self-love

| One · Honesty — **Self-honesty** | Two · Trust — **Self-trust** | Three · Forgiveness — **Self-forgiveness** |
|---|---|---|
| See yourself as you are — on a normal Tuesday, not in a crisis | Keep small promises to yourself | Failure without collapse |

| Cost without it: every other trait leaks | Cost without it: small things slide | Cost without it: mistakes or unfavourable outcomes keep derailing you |

### Traits of good PMs — a working list
1. **High agency** — an internal locus of control.
2. **Product sense** — intuition, not just optimisation.
3. **Judgement** — knowing which call to make, and when.
4. **Curiosity & empathy** — two threads, kept spinning.

### Ownership of effort — the importance of Bhagavad Gita 2.47

**The verse:**
> कर्मण्येवाधिकारस्ते मा फलेषु कदाचन।
> मा कर्मफलहेतुर्भूर्मा ते सङ्गोऽस्त्वकर्मणि॥ ४७॥
> *Karmaṇyevādhikāraste mā phaleṣu kadācana, mā karmaphalaheturbhūrmā te saṅgo'stvakarmaṇi.*

**Translation:** "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself the cause of the results of your activities, nor be attached to inaction."

**Key teachings:**
- **Right to action, not results** — you control your effort, focus and dedication; the final outcome depends on many external factors.
- **No attachment to outcomes** — constant craving for rewards or fear of failure creates anxiety, stress and ego-driven bondage.
- **Do not claim doership** — energy, capability and universal laws drive results; you are an instrument rather than the absolute proprietor of the outcome.
- **Avoid inaction (akarma)** — frustration with outcomes should not lead to laziness, passivity or abandoning responsibilities.

*"The second half is where most readings stop."*

### What 2.47 does at work — four reframings
1. **Action stays** — doing the work is non-negotiable. The verse rejects inaction. Show up, open the doc, run the meeting, ship the draft. The act is yours.
2. **Outcome is not the work** — praise or blame, promotion or reorg, exit or stay: the result lands where it lands. You are not a vending machine.
3. **Motivation shifts — from outcome to craft.** Replace "raise the number" with "study the craft". A craftsman shows up for the work, not the applause it might attract.
4. **Burnout softens** — the work stays sharp; the operator heals. Not detachment as coldness, but the removal of a particular anxiety. The work tightens under steadier hands.

> It is honest about how much of the work stays in your hands — and how little of the result does.

### Three ways to misread 2.47
| Misreading | Why it's wrong | Counter |
|---|---|---|
| **01 · Do nothing** — "Don't attach to outcomes, so why bother." | The second half explicitly outlaws inaction; quietism isn't in the line | Act. Repeatedly. Without bargaining for the result each time. |
| **02 · Don't care about anything** | Detachment read coldly becomes indifference — about the code, the user, the colleague who picked up the bug. That isn't yogic, or even adult | Care — a lot — about the work and the people. Let the rewards be the rewards. |
| **03 · Refuse feedback** — "ignore what happened" | The verse is about what the actor owes the work, not what they owe themselves about being right | Learn from the last result; don't be held hostage by it. |

The verse is a small toolkit — and a small guardrail.

**Start loving yourself. Thank you.**
