## 2. Recap: Bhavishya case & how the persona process should run

Recap of the Session 1 case: Bhavishya Pension Labs (micro-pension app, ₹50 minimum UPI deposits, PFRDA-regulated). 1,20,000 sign-ups in August 2026, only 25,320 (21.1%) deposited within 30 days. The board's market description ("informal-sector workers aged 22–45 who want a simple way to save for retirement") was unbuildable. Characters: Chaitali Dasgupta (Lead Researcher), Dev Chauhan (Head of Growth), Bela Dutta (Head of Engineering), Charvi Bansal (Head of Product).

### The persona-building process — five steps

1. **Observe** — 41 interviews, data
2. **Extract** — behaviour variables
3. **Choose what matters here** ← *"the step usually left unspoken"*
4. **Cluster** — and test differences
5. **Write persona** — tied to a decision

- **How it *should* run:** every stakeholder (research, growth, engineering, product) is involved across the steps — especially in step 3, choosing which variables matter, so the resulting personas are shared and tied to decisions.
- **How it *could* run (and the implications):** each step handled by one person in isolation — the research lead does the work alone and hands over a finished artefact. The risk: the "what matters here" choice is made implicitly by one person, and the others don't own or trust the personas.

## 3. Goal of this session

> **To find out whether anybody has the problem — before you spend weeks solving it.**

1. **A way to ask that works** — questions about the past, from people with nothing invested in your feelings.
2. **A problem, not a feature request** — written so that a stranger could tell you it was wrong.
3. **A rough sense of how big** — built from the bottom up, in numbers you could defend.

## 4. Talking to people without being lied to

> Nobody is trying to mislead you. They are being kind, they are guessing about their own future, and you are asking the wrong questions.

### Why your first ten conversations will be useless
**Everyone will tell you your idea is good. Nobody is lying.** People are generous, want the conversation to go well, and are genuinely bad at predicting their own behaviour. Ask for an opinion about the future and you get a kind guess.

### The Mom Test — three rules that fix most of it
*(Rob Fitzpatrick, The Mom Test — "ninety pages, and the one book I would actually make time for this term.")*

1. **Talk about their life, not your idea.** The moment you describe what you are building, you have told them what answer you want — and they will give it to you.
2. **Ask about the past, not the future.** "What did you do last time?" has an answer. "Would you use this?" has only a guess wearing the clothes of an answer.
3. **Talk less. Specifics, not generalities.** If you're talking more than a third of the time, you're presenting, not learning. "Usually" means they're summarising — ask for the last time.

### The same conversation, two ways

| Questions that get you a compliment | Questions that get you a fact |
|---|---|
| A. "Would you use an app that did X?" | A. "Tell me about the last time that happened." |
| B. "Do you think this is a good idea?" | B. "What did you do about it?" |
| C. "Would you pay ₹500 a month for it?" | C. "What did that cost you — time or money?" |
| D. "How often does this problem happen?" | D. "What have you already tried, and why did you stop?" |
| E. "What features would you want?" | E. "Who else was involved when it went wrong?" |

The left column asks for a **prediction**; the right asks for a **memory**.
**Note pair D:** "How often does this happen?" sounds factual but isn't — it invites an estimate, and estimates are shaped by how interesting the conversation is.

### Three things that feel like data — and are not

| False signal | Example | Why it's worthless |
|---|---|---|
| **Compliments** | "That's a great idea." | Tells you they are polite. Write it nowhere. |
| **Generalities** | "I usually…", "I always…" | A summary, smoothed of the detail you needed. |
| **Promises about the future** | "I'd definitely buy that." | The cheapest sentence in any language. |

**What counts instead:** things that already happened and already cost them something. A person who has already paid for a bad solution, built a spreadsheet, hired someone or changed their routine has shown demand with behaviour — worth fifty enthusiastic yeses, and the only evidence that survives a sceptical reader.

## 5. Running the conversations

### How to start one with a stranger — student playbook
1. **Ask for help, not for an opinion.** "I'm a student trying to understand how shops handle payments. Could I ask you about your week?" — not "can I show you my idea?"
2. **Name the time it will take, and keep to it.** Fifteen minutes. People say yes to fifteen and resent twenty-five.
3. **Open with the last time, not the topic.** "Tell me about yesterday evening after you closed" beats "do you have problems with payments?"
4. **Shut up after you ask.** The silence is where the real answer lives. Count to five before you fill it.

> You are not selling anything and you are not validating anything. You are being curious about somebody's Tuesday.

### What to write down

| ✗ Not worth the ink | ✓ Write these verbatim |
|---|---|
| Your summary of what they meant | The exact words they used for the problem |
| Their opinion of your idea | What they did, with dates and durations |
| "Seemed interested" | What it cost them — money, hours, a customer |
| Anything you can reconstruct later | The thing they said that surprised you |

**Their words, not yours.** You're collecting language as well as facts. The words a customer uses for their own problem are the words that belong on your landing page in Session 8. Paraphrase them now and you'll spend three months writing copy that sounds like a company.

### Four ways you will lead the witness
- **A. Confirmation** — you remember the two people who agreed and forget the six who shrugged.
- **B. Leading the question** — "So that's frustrating, isn't it?" — you've written their answer for them.
- **C. The wrong people** — friends, classmates, whoever was easiest to reach. All of them are kind.
- **D. Your existing idea** — you went in wanting one answer, and conversations are good at providing it.

**Cheapest defence:** write down what you expect to hear *before* you go. Afterwards you can tell whether you learned something or just collected agreement. Two minutes; the only bias correction that reliably works on yourself.

### How many conversations, and with whom
- **First five, then five more.** The first five teach you what to ask; the second five have the answers.
- **When to stop:** when it repeats — when the fourth person in a row tells you nothing new, that segment is done.
- **Who to avoid:** nobody you know. A friend can't give you a real no; classmates are the worst sample available.
- **Where they are:** where they already gather — the market, the clinic waiting room, the apartment group, the shop at 8 pm.

Ten good conversations in a fortnight is entirely achievable — and more than most funded startups manage.

## 6. From N conversations to 1 pattern

1. **One note per observation, in their words.** Not one note per person — a single interview might produce fifteen notes or two.
2. **Group them without a plan.** Put like with like on a wall/table. Don't name groups until they exist.
3. **Name the group last, from the notes inside it.** If you can't name it without stretching, it's two groups or noise.
4. **Quote first, claim second.** Every claim carries the line someone actually said. *No quote, no claim.*

A pattern appearing in nine of twelve conversations is a **finding**. One that appears in one or two is a **hypothesis** and should be labelled as one.

### A note on using AI for this

| Genuinely good at | Will quietly ruin your work |
|---|---|
| + Transcribing and tidying your notes | − Inventing a plausible pattern from thin data |
| + Suggesting groupings you didn't see | − Smoothing their words into your words |
| + Finding the quote you half-remember | − Confident synthesis of four chats |
| + Drafting the write-up once you have the pattern | − Letting nobody on the team read the transcripts |

> "Synthesis of nothing is nothing — and it reads beautifully."

Use AI *after* the grouping, not instead of it. At least one human on the team should read every transcript end to end — the thing you need is usually the aside, not the theme.

### Discovery is not a phase you finish. It is a habit you keep.
Teams treat research as week two of the project. Teams that do well are still talking to three people a week in week five — including after they've decided what to build.

## 7. From conversations to a problem

> **A problem is not a missing feature. It is somebody, in a situation, prevented from doing something, at a cost you can name.**

### The commonest mistake: "They need a dashboard" is not a problem
It is your solution, wearing a disguise. A problem sits underneath every solution. If you describe what to build before you can name the problem, you are designing, not discovering.

### What a problem statement has in it — five elements
1. **Somebody specific, in a specific situation** — not "people like me"; a real shopkeeper on a real Tuesday, in a real pin code.
2. **Something they were trying to do** — the intended outcome, in their words, not your interpretation.
3. **An obstacle, named** — "No single app shows all of it" is the obstacle. A rebranded obstacle is not a problem.
4. **A cost that has a number** — hours, rupees, one customer. Without a number, the cost is rhetoric.
5. **A workaround that shows the budget** — a spreadsheet, an extra hire, a paper day-book: evidence the pain is paid for.

**If you cannot name one of these five, you have a request, not a problem.**

### The same problem, written twice
- ❌ **Weak:** "Small retailers lack visibility into their finances and need a better way to manage their payments digitally."
- ✅ **Strong enough to argue with:** "Shop owners who take payments on three different apps **[who, when]** spend 30–45 minutes after closing, three or four evenings a week, matching those payments against a paper day-book — **because no single app shows them all of it [obstacle]**. Getting it wrong once cost one of them **a ₹4,000 dispute she could not prove [cost, with a number]**. Today they **do it by hand [workaround]**, and two of the five we met have simply stopped checking."

*(Illustrative, built from the Session 1 Lakshmi example. Yours comes from people you actually met.)*

### Four problems that look good and are not

| Type | Example | Why it fails |
|---|---|---|
| **The feature dressed as problem** | "We need a dashboard" | Embeds your solution. Strip the noun and find the obstacle underneath, or pick another. |
| **One without a face** | "People are busy" | Nobody wakes up to be "people". Without a person, a place, a moment, it won't move a team. |
| **Without a number** | "It costs time" | How much, how often, paid for by whom? Anything else leaves room to dismiss it. |
| **That needs you to explain it** | "It's an experience problem" | If you have to put it in a slide, you can't put it in a sentence a stranger will argue with. |

## 8. Which problem to pick

### Frequency × severity grid (for *one person*, not the market)

| | **Mild** | **Painful** |
|---|---|---|
| **Frequent** | A real market, and a crowded one — you will compete on polish | **Start here.** People are already paying something to avoid it |
| **Rare** | Nobody will change anything. Walk away | Insurance, not a habit. Hard to build a product on |

Both axes are about one person. A problem a million people have once a decade is **not** a frequent problem.

### A third question the grid doesn't ask — reachability
**Can you actually get to forty of these people in the next fortnight?**
- **A. They gather somewhere** — a market, a building, a group, a clinic: a place you can physically go.
- **B. Or somebody can introduce you** — one person who knows thirty of them is worth a month of cold outreach.
- **× If neither is true** — it may be a real problem, and the wrong one for a project.

Reachability is not a compromise on ambition; it is the difference between a project with evidence and one with assertions.

### Not everyone with the problem has it equally — finding the beachhead
1. **Who has it most often** — three payment apps, not one; six evenings a week, not two. Volume concentrates pain.
2. **Who it costs most** — the one-person shop loses their own evening; the five-person shop loses a salary.
3. **Who has already tried to fix it** — they've shown you the budget and told you what didn't work.
4. **Who you can reach this fortnight** — the constraint that decides which of the above you actually serve.

**Your beachhead is the overlap of all four.** It will feel uncomfortably narrow — that's the point.

## 9. Is it big enough? Sizing bottom-up

Three numbers, built from the bottom up, defensible to someone who doesn't want to believe you.

### Three numbers, and where each comes from

| Number | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| People with the problem | **19** | Of 40 shops walked in three pin codes, 19 reconcile three or more apps by hand |
| Times a week | **3–4** | Evenings shopkeepers reconcile by hand, by their own count |
| Hours per week | **~2** | Bottom up — anchored to people you talked to, not a market report |

Multiply by walk-throughs and margins of error before you publish. Three defensible numbers beat one number defended by you alone.

### Two ways to size it — only one is evidence

| Top-down | Bottom-up |
|---|---|
| "The Indian retail market is $1.2 trillion" | "There are ~400 shops in these 3 pin codes" |
| "There are 60 million small businesses" | "We walked 40. 26 take 3+ payment apps" |
| "If we capture just 0.1%…" | "Of those, 19 reconcile by hand, 3–4 nights a week" |
| — | "At 35 minutes, that is ~2 hours a week each" |
| **Impressive, unfalsifiable, worth nothing. Discounted by every reader.** | **Small, checkable, hard to wave away.** |

The bottom-up number is always smaller, and it's the only one that tells you what to do on Monday morning.

### What that number decides (for a 4–5 week project)
- **Too small to carry a project** — a few dozen people, once a quarter, saving minutes. Real, but not a product. Widen or move.
- **Enough for five weeks** — a few hundred reachable people with a weekly cost you can state. **Start here.**
- **Suspiciously large** — if your bottom-up number is enormous, you've made an unchecked assumption. Find it.

You're not deciding whether to raise money; you're deciding whether to spend the next five weeks on this.

## 10. Where surveys fit

| Surveys CANNOT | Surveys CAN |
|---|---|
| × Discover a problem you hadn't thought of | ✓ Count something you already found by talking |
| × Tell you why anybody answered that way | ✓ Check whether a pattern holds more widely |
| × Rescue you from not having talked to anyone | ✓ Sort respondents so you can go interview some |
| | ✓ Produce one number for the submission |

**Qualitative first, then quantitative. Numbers confirm; they don't discover.** A survey written before interviewing anyone asks the questions you already had, in words you already used — it comes back tidy and tells you nothing.

### Qual ↔ Quant cycle (diagram)
- **Qualitative:** 5–8 interviews, open prompts → finds *what, why, in whose words*.
- **Quantitative:** survey/instrumentation, closed questions → finds *how many, how often*.
- Qual **supplies vocabulary** for quant; quant results **send you back to interviews** when a number surprises you.
- *"The two modes are sequential and cyclical, not competing."*

## 11. Bonus: real research artefacts

- **Instructions by the lead researcher** — a real moderator brief (Project Portafoglio, Verizon, September '21). Instructions for the moderator include:
  - Make sure the conversation and screens shared by the respondent are recorded.
  - Encourage respondents to talk in detail; don't let them restrict opinions to "yes/no", "good/bad" — ask for details and probe until they give reasons. *Ladder it* to their emotional needs.
  - Be mindful of body language and facial expressions; take notes.
  - Ensure questions aren't producing primed responses or creating bias.
  - **Objective of the study:** understand expectations, content and organisational preferences with respect to folders on the user's screens.
- **A recording of a user conversation** — played in class (video).

## 12. Three key learnings

1. **Ask about the past, never about the future.** People are generous and unreliable about what they *would* do, and accurate about what they *did*. Change the questions, not the people.
2. **A problem is not a missing feature.** If your statement is still true when your company doesn't exist, it's a problem. If it contains a thing you'd build, start again.
3. **Behaviour beats agreement, every time.** A spreadsheet somebody built to cope is worth fifty people telling you it's a great idea — and it's the only evidence that survives a sceptic.

"These three are what makes Session 2 different from a pitch deck."

## 14. In-class drill & team commitment

### Form team & commit
Ten minutes. Every team says its problem out loud, writes it down, and hands it in. Session 3 builds against what you write. (A doc of faculty-proposed sample ideas is available to pick from: `Faculty_proposed_Ideas.docx`.)

### Drill — the sheet
**Team · People spoken to so far**
1. Who, and when it bites
2. What they were trying to do
3. What got in the way
4. What it cost them
5. Their workaround today
6. How many, how often

Each cell holds three lines. Anything you can't fill in is something you need to ask one more person about.

### Drill — the swap
You have another team's sheet; you may not ask them anything. Answer in writing:
1. **Is there a solution hiding in there?** Underline any phrase that assumes what gets built: "needs a way to…", "lacks visibility", "should be able to see…".
2. **What is the strongest evidence, and the weakest?** Name one clause you believe and one you don't. If every clause is equally soft, say so.

Then take two answers from the room — the hidden solutions, not the statements. Everybody has one; finding it in somebody else's writing is how you learn to see it in your own.

### The problem you are taking to Session 3
One paragraph · read it out · hand it in before you leave.
- Team / People spoken to so far
- **The problem, in one paragraph**
- **What we are still assuming**
- **Who, and by when** (will check it)

> If you cannot put it in a paragraph a colleague could disagree with, you are not done yet.
