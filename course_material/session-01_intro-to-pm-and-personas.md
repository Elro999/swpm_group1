**"Do not form your team around an idea. Form it around people you can disagree with."**

## 7. Origins of product management

The role emerged in five stages:

### 1. 1931 — The brand man (Procter & Gamble)
- A short memo at P&G names a job no company had a word for.
- **Neil McElroy**, then a promotions manager, argues for "brand men" — single owners accountable for one brand end-to-end: its sales data, advertising and market.
- The novelty isn't a function; it is **one person holding the commercial outcome — and being held to it.**
- McElroy later ran P&G and became US Secretary of Defense.

### 2. 1940s–'50s — Engineers moved in (Hewlett-Packard)
- The P&G idea adapted for hardware: decisions close to the people building the thing.
- HP's version: *"Product decisions move down into small divisions, so the responsible person sits with the engineers — not above them."*
- Before, at P&G, the brand man owned sales data and advertising and sat close to the customer, not the engineers. Now **a product manager sat with the engineers, not above them.**

### 3. Post-war Japan — The chief engineer (Toyota's *shusa*)
- **Influence, not authority.** The *shusa* ("chief engineer") owns a vehicle but doesn't formally own the people making it — total responsibility with very little formal power.
- That asymmetry is still the job's defining feature: influence through knowledge, not reporting lines.
- The lean tradition — Ohno, kanban, "go and see" — kept that posture. Ries's *Lean Startup* (2011) re-enters product through exactly this door.

### 4. 1984 — The software split (Microsoft)
- Microsoft creates the **program manager** role, usually credited to **Jabe Blumenthal** on Excel, for software shipped to mass markets.
- A new role sits between engineering and the user, because the user is no longer a procurement officer but a consumer.
- What changed: software ships to millions; not sales-led any more — engineering needs a translator. This is the direct ancestor of today's tech PM.

### 5. 2001 → — Codification

| Year | Contribution | What it gave the job |
|---|---|---|
| 2001 | Agile Manifesto | A new label — *product owner* — and a backlog to triage |
| 2002 | Google's APM programme | An archetype; Ken Norton's hiring essay shapes the mould |
| 2008 | Cagan's *Inspired* | The role becomes something you can teach, write about, hire against |
| 2011 | Ries's *Lean Startup* | Toyota's posture gets a portable, named practice |

"And AI is doing the same thing again now."

## 8. What product management is — and isn't

### What the job actually is
> **You are accountable for an outcome in a system you do not control.**

### Three things only you own
1. **The problem** — which one we are solving, for whom, and why that one before the others.
2. **The order** — what comes first, what comes later, what never comes — and who gets disappointed.
3. **The definition of done** — what "working" means, specifically enough that someone else can check it.

Everything else is shared, borrowed, or somebody else's.

### Four things the job is comfortably mistaken for
| Misconception | Why it's wrong |
|---|---|
| "CEO of the product" | A CEO can fire people and move budget. You can write a document and be persuasive. |
| The feature factory | Shipping more is not the job. Half of what ships moves nothing — and somebody chose it. |
| The ticket writer | If your week is converting other people's requests into tickets, you are a queue. |
| The visionary | Vision is cheap and everybody has one. What is scarce is being right about the user. |

All four are comfortable because they replace uncertain judgement with a confident activity.

### What you will not have vs. what you can spend

| What you will not have | What you can spend |
|---|---|
| A reporting line to anyone who builds it | **Context** — why this, why now, for whom |
| A vote on the engineering estimate | **Sequence** — what to ask for first |
| The final call on how it is built | **Evidence** — what you went and found out |
| The budget, usually | **Credit** — given away, it comes back |

> "Responsibility without authority is not a flaw in the job. It is the job."

### Who else is in the room

| Role | Accountable for | Which in practice means |
|---|---|---|
| Product manager | What gets built, and why | The problem, the order, the outcome |
| Product marketing | How the world hears about it | Positioning, launch, the sales story |
| Technical program manager | That it actually lands | Dependencies, schedule, risk across teams |
| Project manager | Delivery to plan | Scope, timeline, status — output, not outcome |
| Founder | All of it, with authority | And the ability to change their own mind on a Tuesday |

### The product loop
**Discover** (who, and what hurts) → **Define** (what we would build) → **Build** (and what we won't) → **Measure** (did it work?) → **Learn** → back to Discover, with a better question than you started with.

> "The loop is not a process diagram. It is a claim about how often you are allowed to be wrong."

### Treat your idea as a set of bets
1. **Write down what you believe** — "People like X have problem Y, and would change what they do today to fix it."
2. **Name the one that would hurt most if wrong** — there is always one; it is usually about the customer, not the technology.
3. **Find the cheapest way to find out** — five conversations beats a quarter of building, every single time.
4. **Then change your mind in public** — a team that never revised its belief was not testing it; it was decorating it.

> "You will be wrong. The only question is how expensively you find out."

*(6-minute in-class activity.)*

## 10. Part 2 — Personas

*Designing for and understanding someone who is not you.*

### Pause (60 sec)
"Who uses this product, and what are they trying to get done?" — write your answer on paper before continuing.

### What you'll leave with
1. **Explain** why personas exist and what they change in a team.
2. **Build** a persona from evidence.
3. **Spot** a weak persona — a stereotype, an average, or a self-portrait.

### Defining a persona (layered model)
Concentric layers, from outside in, feeding a product decision:
- **Context:** age, city, device (outer layer)
- **Behaviours:** what they do
- **Attitudes:** what they fear
- **Goals:** what they want (core)

→ **Product decision:** build, price, message, sequence.

### First honest note: *We are not the user.*
Our job is to design for people who think, decide and act differently from us. Listen.

### Why personas matter — three reasons
1. **Empathy** — turns an abstract market into one person the team can picture.
2. **Goals** — focuses on what the person is trying to achieve.
3. **Alignment** — gives engineers, designers and PMs the same customer to argue about.

### Segments say *who*. Personas say *why*.

| | Market segment | Persona |
|---|---|---|
| Built from | Demographics, geography, firm size | Goals, attitudes, behaviours |
| Answers | How many people, and how big is the group? | What do they want, and what stops them? |
| Drives | Sizing and targeting | Design and prioritisation decisions |

### Anatomy of a persona — five fields, in this order
1. **Context** — who they are, and what surrounds their day.
2. **Goals** — the job they are trying to get done.
3. **Attitudes** — what they believe, fear or value.
4. **Behaviours** — what they actually do, step by step.
5. **Pains & gains** — what makes the job hard, and what would delight them.

### The most common failure: *An average is not a person.*
"The average user" describes the middle of many people — nobody in your research actually looks like this. A persona is one specific (composite) person.

### Build from evidence — three steps
Not imagination. Evidence — collect, cluster, personify.

| Step | What | Detail |
|---|---|---|
| 01 Collect | Interviews, observation, support tickets | Not opinions |
| 02 Cluster | Group people by the goals and struggles they share | Not by age or income → three to six clusters |
| 03 Personify | Write each cluster as one person | Output: a persona on paper |

Time: weeks, not hours.

### Three ways a persona goes wrong
| Failure | Description | Smell test |
|---|---|---|
| **Stereotype** | A confident caricature of someone no one has met — built on assumption, not observation | Could anyone you interviewed be it? |
| **Average** | A smoothed-over version of everyone — the centre of mass instead of a person | Could nobody be offended? |
| **Self-portrait** | The team describing itself | Could you swap in yourself? |

### Living with personas — two failure modes
1. **Research debt** — personas go stale.
2. **AI synthesis** — AI can invent them.

A persona is a **hypothesis about people**. Check it against new evidence, or it quietly turns into fiction.

### The anti-persona
Define who you will **not** serve. An anti-persona names the users you will deliberately not design for. It keeps the product sharp and makes trade-offs visible.
**The question:** *Who are we explicitly not building for?*

## 11. Case study: Bhavishya Pension Labs

### Situation
Bhavishya runs a **micro-pension app**: users deposit small amounts — as little as ₹50 at a time — into a pension account regulated under the **PFRDA** framework, paying via **UPI**.

- August 2026: **1,20,000** new sign-ups.
- Only **25,320 (21.1%)** made a deposit within thirty days.
- The board deck's market description: *"Informal-sector workers aged 22 to 45 who want a simple way to save for retirement."* — **Nobody could build from it.**

### Characters (Bhavishya Pension Labs, Bengaluru)

| Character | Role | Trying to launch / ship | Stand / evidence | Quote | Owns |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Chaitali Dasgupta** | Lead Researcher | Three evidence-backed personas, each tied to a decision, not a slide | 41 interviews in Pune, Kanpur and Hubballi, tested against the August deposit cohort | "Age and city told me almost nothing. Income rhythm told me everything." | Interviews & personas |
| **Dev Chauhan** | Head of Growth | A salary-day deposit reminder pushed to the entire base, on a ₹90 lakh Q3 budget | Spend on reach. One reminder, for everyone, before the quarter closes | "The budget is for growth. Growth means more people depositing, not more people signing up." | Acquisition mix & budget |
| **Bela Dutta** | Head of Engineering | Exactly one major feature, live and stable, in the ten days before the committee meets | Constraint: one feature is possible; the wrong one costs the whole quarter | "I can ship one thing. Tell me which thing, and tell me why that one." | Build capacity & delivery |
| **Charvi Bansal** | Head of Product | One major feature, chosen on evidence, before the 18 September product committee | Stop describing the market. Give it faces, and let the deposit data decide | "A market description tells us whom to count; a persona tells us what to change." | The September decision |

### The three evidence-backed personas

| | **Charu** | **Dinesh** | **Bhagwati** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who | Freelance designer · 31 · Bengaluru | Delivery rider · 28 · Pune | Shop & household accounts · 44 · Hubballi |
| Goal | Grow her retirement savings without locking away money she might need | Set aside a little every day, before the cash in his pocket disappears | Put money aside in a good season, for the lean months and for old age |
| Obstacle | Invoices land unpredictably; she distrusts anything that traps her cash | Paid daily in cash; no salary day, no single moment that triggers a deposit | Household and shop income is lumpy and seasonal; no predictable month exists |
| 30-day deposit rate | **30.0%** | **18.0%** | **12.5%** |
| Sign-ups | 42,000 | 54,000 | 24,000 |
| Typical monthly deposit | ₹1,500 | ₹600 | ₹2,000 |
| **Decision** | **Primary persona: 61.5% of deposit value** | End-of-shift micro-nudge, ₹50 default | Seasonal lump-sum prompt, not a monthly one |

**Lesson:** the persona that matters is defined by **income rhythm**, not age or city. A single "salary-day reminder for everyone" (Dev's plan) fits none of them — Dinesh has no salary day and Bhagwati has no predictable month. Each persona maps to a *different* product decision.

## 12. Personas in practice

### Four things people quietly muddle together
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Segment** | A group that behaves alike. "Shop owners who already take digital payments." |
| **Persona** | One person who stands for that group, specific enough to settle arguments. |
| **Buyer** | The one who pays. Often not the one who uses it — and cares about different things. |
| **User** | The one who uses it. |

> "Where the buyer and the user are different people, most products quietly serve the buyer."

### What a persona is actually for
| It is NOT | It IS |
|---|---|
| A poster for the wall | A decision tool, used in arguments |
| A demographic profile with a stock photo | A record of what you learned from real people |
| A marketing asset | A way to say no with a reason |
| Something you write once and never open | Something you revise when you learn you were wrong |

**The test:** can two people on your team use it to disagree productively?

### What goes into one that actually works — six elements
1. **The situation** — where they are and what is going on around them when it matters. Time, place, pressure.
2. **The job** — what they are trying to get done, in their words, not yours.
3. **What they do today** — the actual workaround: notebook, spreadsheet, phone call, the cousin.
4. **The trigger** — what made them start looking.
5. **What would make them switch** — and what it would cost them: effort, money, habit, risk, embarrassment.
6. **What would make them leave** — the one thing that, if it happened twice, ends it.

Notice what is **not** on the list: age, income, city, education. Demographics describe a person; these six describe a **situation**.

### The same person, written twice
- ❌ **Demographic version:** "Lakshmi, 34, urban professional. Tech-savvy, time-poor, values convenience and quality. Enjoys travel and online shopping. Wants a seamless experience."
- ✅ **Decision-tool version:** "Lakshmi runs a two-chair salon. Three evenings a week she reconciles digital payments against her notebook after closing, and it takes forty minutes. She started doing it the month a customer disputed a payment and she could not prove it. She would switch for something that matched payments to her bookings automatically — but not if it meant retyping her customer list. She would leave if it ever showed a payment that had not arrived."
  - ✓ Names the situation ✓ Names the workaround ✓ Names the trigger ✓ Names what ends it

### Exercise: build 2 personas for Swiggy Instamart (9 min)
**Template — eight rows (the eighth is the most important):**

| # | Field | Prompt |
|---|---|---|
| 01 | Name and line | A realistic first name, one sentence on who they are |
| 02 | Role, setting, constraints | Where they are, what they own, what limits them |
| 03 | Goal: the job | What they are trying to get done, in their words |
| 04 | Workaround today | What they do instead, and what it costs them |
| 05 | Attitudes | What they value, fear or assume about this work |
| 06 | Behaviours | What they actually do, step by step |
| 07 | Pains & gains | What makes the job hard, and what would delight them |
| 08 | **Evidence** | **MOST IMPORTANT** — what real data backs this |

### Three key learnings (Personas block)
1. A persona is a **shared, evidence-based picture of one person** — not a category, not a clipping.
2. **Goals and behaviours matter more** than averages and demographics.
3. **Revisit, verify, and say who you are not building for.**

## 13. Product sense

> The ability to take a product apart and say why each decision was made — in terms somebody could disagree with.
> **It is not taste. It is being able to say why.**

### Six questions, asked in this order
1. **Who is this for?** Not "everyone". Who does it clearly serve first — and who does it tolerate?
2. **What job are they hiring it for?** What were they doing before this existed? That is the real competitor.
3. **What does it optimise for?** Watch the defaults. The default is the decision, repeated a million times.
4. **What does it deliberately not do?**
5. **How does it make money?** And from whom. If the user is not paying, somebody else is choosing.
6. **What would break it?** A behaviour change, a cost, a platform, a competitor who gives it away.

### The question nobody asks
> "What does this product refuse to do — and who did it give up to stay that way?"

| Refusal | Trade-off |
|---|---|
| A short menu | Fewer choices, faster decisions — the customer who wanted the other thing goes elsewhere |
| No free tier | A smaller top of funnel, better conversations, a support load you can actually carry |
| One city only | A worse press release, and a density that makes the whole thing work at all |

"A product that does everything was designed by nobody in particular."

### Every product decision costs somebody something
1. **Make it simpler** → the power user loses the control they built their workflow around.
2. **Make it faster** → something gets cached, approximated or dropped; someone notices the one you dropped.
3. **Make it cheaper** → either the margin or the service goes; both are visible within a quarter.
4. **Make it safer** → another step, another confirmation — and a measurable share of people give up.

> "If you cannot name who loses, you have not finished thinking — you have only finished deciding."

### Exercise: Name six. Pick one.
Zerodha · Zepto · CRED · Rapido · IRCTC · WhatsApp. Good answers name a feature the product could obviously have built and **chose not to**.

## 14. The customer and the persona — the drill

> **The word to stop using:** "'Users' is not a customer. It is a way of avoiding one."

### Drill: build one persona (15 min)
| Time | Step | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| 2 min | Pick a real person one of you knows | Not a type — a person with a name, whom one of you could phone this evening |
| 7 min | Fill the six boxes | Where you don't know, write "don't know" — do not invent |
| 4 min | Swap with the next table | They may not ask you anything; they answer two questions in writing |
| 2 min | Take it back | Circle every box that turned out to be a guess — that list is your Session 2 homework |

> "'Don't know' is the most valuable thing you can write today. It is the only honest output of a first attempt."

**The sheet (six cells):** Name (a real person) · Who on your team knows them · 1. The situation · 2. The job · 3. What they do today · 4. The trigger · 5. What would make them switch · 6. What would make them leave.

### The swap — two questions answered in writing
1. **Could you pick this person out of a crowd of similar people?** If the description would fit a hundred people equally well, say so.
2. **Which box is a guess dressed up as a fact?** Be specific and blunt — they have a week to go find out, and a polite read is worth nothing.

Then take two answers from the room — not the personas, the guesses.

## 17. Extra slides

### B2B and B2C are different jobs

| | Consumer (B2C) | Business (B2B) |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | The person using it | A committee — none of whom use it daily |
| How they find you | Search, stores, a friend, an ad | A salesperson, a conference, a procurement list |
| How long it takes | Seconds to days | Three months to two years |
| What kills it | They stop opening it | It doesn't get renewed — and nobody tells you why |
| What they forgive | A lot, if the thing is delightful | Very little, if it breaks a process |

### How to ask without being lied to

| Questions that get you an opinion (future) | Questions that get you a fact (past) |
|---|---|
| "Would you use something that does X?" | "Tell me about the last time that happened." |
| "Do you think this is a good idea?" | "What did you do about it?" |
| "Would you pay for this?" | "What did that cost you — time or money?" |
| "How often do you have this problem?" | "What have you already tried?" |

> People are generous and unreliable about what they *would* do, and accurate about what they *did*. Ask only about the past, and about specific occasions rather than habits.
