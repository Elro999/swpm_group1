## **Problem hypotheses**

_Framework: The [type] of [customer] are most frustrated about [job to be done] because [problem]._

### **Problem Hypothesis 1: Explaining my own decision**

_The ISB placement candidates who prepare behavioural answers from team projects are most frustrated about explaining their own decision in a short answer because they spend the first minutes on context and shared work, so the listener has to interrupt to ask which decision was theirs._

Cost reported: SUR-1 spoke for almost four minutes (I1-6) and spent about 45 minutes reworking the answer (I112); SUR-3 spent about 90 minutes across the mock, his roommate and his notes (I3-14).

### **Problem Hypothesis 2: Making experience relevant to an unfamiliar listener**

_The career switchers and domain specialists preparing at ISB are most frustrated about making their past work relevant to a listener outside their domain because their explanations assume knowledge the listener lacks, while general rewrites add ownership or impact they cannot defend._

Cost reported: SUR-2 lost about an hour recovering evidence (I2-22); SUR-3 would not upload his incident report to an AI tool (I3-18).

### **Problem Hypothesis 3: Knowing which feedback to act on**

_The ISB candidates who revise answers using several reviewers and tools are most frustrated about knowing which changes improve an answer because the advice is conflicting or vague, the notes end up in several places, and repeating the same story does not test whether the change helped._

Cost reported: repeated edits and reversals (I1-30, I3-22), an unused feedback note (I2-28), and no replay of a group session (I4-14). Not measured in time.

### **Additional problem hypothesis**

### **Problem Hypothesis 4: Contributing in a live group discussion**

_The ISB candidates who hesitate in group discussions are most frustrated about contributing a useful point at the right moment because solo preparation does not reproduce interruptions, entry timing or other speakers._

Only SUR-4 raised this (1 of 5; I4-6, I4-12), so it is a single-interview theme and may need a different intervention from a personal-answer companion.

## **Implications and limits**

**Implications.** H1 and H2 are the strongest: their themes recur across several interviews (C1, C2, C4) and cost real time each round. H3 recurs too (C3, C9) but is mild on its own, so it works better as support for the other two. H4 came from one interview (C11, C12 point the same way but are separate themes) and should be tested separately. SUR-5 is a reminder that a candidate with a reliable peer group and a method that works may not need a new tool.

**Limits.** Five interviews cannot show how widespread a problem is, and every participant is an ISB student, so the sample reflects one cohort and not all job seekers (the class calls this mirror profiling). Costs are what interviewees reported, mostly in minutes; no interview or offer outcome was measured. The next step is to repeat this with a wider mix of candidates and to test H1 with a fresh listener who must name the candidate's decision before and after a revision.

### **A.1.1 How the interview candidates were selected**

All five interviewees are ISB PGP classmates, each reached through an introduction from another classmate rather than from my own close circle, to keep one degree of separation and limit mirror profiling. I selected them to vary on three things:

- **Prior field:** analytics, software development, manufacturing operations, consumer sales, boutique consulting.

- **Target role:** consulting, product management with RCA, general management, and group discussion with personal interview.

- **How they prepare:** a heavy reviser, a candidate who depends on peer mocks, a candidate who depends on a live group, and a candidate who is largely satisfied with his current method.

I deliberately included SUR-5, who is satisfied with his current method, so the sample could contradict the idea rather than only confirm it. Each conversation lasted about six to seven minutes, was recorded on a voice recorder app with the interviewee's consent, and was transcribed in full. The conversation followed the class rules: start from the last practice session, ask what happened next and what they did about it, avoid hypothetical questions about the product, and do not pitch.
