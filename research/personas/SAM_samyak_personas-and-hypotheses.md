# **<mark>Assignment 1: Personas and Problem Hypotheses</mark>**

# **Section 1.1: Personas**

I interviewed five people and clustered what they said by the goals and struggles they share. 2 personas came through clearly, and one group came out as an anti-persona. Each persona is anchored on the one or two interviewees whose situation it actually describes.

**Persona 1: Kiran Mehta, the experienced storyteller who loses it in delivery**

|Name|Kiran Mehta, 31, around 8 years of work experience, currently doing an MBA<br>and interviewing for senior roles through placements. What defines him is the<br>situation: He already has the stories but loses them in live delivery.|
|---|---|
|Tag line / Quote|“I know the complete story, but does the delivery actually bring out the fact that I<br>want to bring out?”|
|Key goals and<br>motivations|1. Convert interviews for senior roles, where rambling “might not be expected of<br>a senior professional”.<br>2. Get more out of a handful of strong stories by changing which part he<br>emphasises (leadership, influencing without authority) based on the question.<br>3. Practise in a way that tests him rather than teaches him. He is in a “delivering<br>phase” now, not a “learning phase”.|
|Environment operating<br>in|A packed MBA schedule (classes, pre-reads, case prep) with interviews coming<br>in bursts. Prep is late and triggered by a specific interview: the day before, or the<br>same morning. Interviews are often over video. Peers, family and ex-colleagues<br>with MBA backgrounds are around, but not always available.|
|Challenges|1. Rambles or rushes once he is live and only notices midway (the interviewer<br>cutting in with “what happened next?”) or after the interview.<br>2. Cannot tell before the interview whether an answer actually lands. He knows<br>feedback from people is better.<br>3. Misreads what a question is really assessing and answers the first thing that<br>comes to mind.<br>4. Paid counsellors are too expensive to use again, and people are not available at<br>the hours he prepares.|
|Tools currently used|Company website and a conversation with someone who has worked there; his<br>resume; MBA peers, family and ex-colleagues for feedback (intended more than<br>done); a paid admissions counsellor in the past, which he now finds too<br>expensive. He has not tried AI tools yet, but is open to them.|
|Trigger to switch|An interview coming up, plus something available at his hours that points out<br>what was right and what was wrong, and lets him record and play back his own<br>answers.|
|What would make him<br>leave|Feedback that is generic and ignores his own story, or that misses the delivery<br>cues (pace, eye contact, hand movement) he feels only a human picks up.|

**Persona 2: Arnav Rao, the career mover who has to explain the “why”**

|Name|Arnav Rao, 27, 3 to 4 years in a specialist field now applying for roles in a new<br>country or a new direction.|
|---|---|
|Tag line / Quote|“The answer is right there, and if you would have presented it in a better way,<br>you could have had a chance.”|
|Key goals and<br>motivations|1. Explain his biggest choices (leaving a job, moving countries, changing<br>direction) convincingly every time, not just on good days.<br>2. Build a clear “why this job” story that connects his experience to where the<br>company is heading.<br>3. Sound like himself. To quote: “your answer should be based on your<br>experience and your background, and not a generalized” answer.|
|Environment operating<br>in|Working or studying alongside the job search, with several interviews a year and<br>full-day interview loops. Prep ranges from a day or two to a couple of weeks.<br>Interviewers almost never explain a rejection: they “hire you if they think you're a<br>fit and otherwise the conversation ends there”. He leans on a trusted friend,<br>alumni and current employees for direction.|
|Challenges|1. Confidence varies from day to day. The same “why did you move?” question<br>goes well on some days and badly on others.<br>2. Building the “introduce yourself” and “why this job” narrative takes a lot of<br>research and time.<br>3. Gets almost no feedback from interviewers. One brief comment (“you don't<br>seem full of energy”) is all he has to go on, and it sticks.<br>4. Finds it hard to put what is in his head into words.<br>5. AI tools and online sample answers feel generic.|

|Trigger to switch|An interview season or a second round coming up, or failing the same question<br>more than once.|
|What would make him<br>leave|If his answers start sounding like everyone else's, or the tool rewrites his story<br>instead of helping him say it better.|

## **Anti-persona: the technical-round grinder**

Candidates preparing for knowledge-heavy rounds, such as DSA and system design (SAM-3) or general knowledge in stress interviews (SAM-2), have a different job. Their struggle is recall and coverage, not explaining their own experience. They are already served well by LeetCode, upvoted sample solutions and articles written by senior engineers. Furthermore, SAM-3 was clear that “sound like everyone else” is not a problem in a technical interview. Hence, I will not design for knowledge recall.

## **Problem Hypothesis 1**

<mark>The</mark> **<mark>candidates who have relevant experience but struggle to deliver it concisely in a live interview (most acutely, experienced professionals going through MBA placements)</mark>** <mark>are most frustrated about</mark> **<mark>delivering their own stories concisely in the interview</mark>** <mark>because they have no quick, always-available way to test how an answer actually lands before the real interview. Human feedback is either not available at their prep hours or too expensive, so they end up skipping it.</mark>

## **Problem Hypothesis 2**

<mark>The</mark> **<mark>early-career professionals who are changing country, field or direction</mark>** <mark>are most frustrated about</mark> **<mark>explaining why they are the right fit for the role, and why they made their choices, with confidence</mark>** <mark>because generic AI and online answers do not reflect their own background, and interviewers almost never tell them what went wrong, so they are left guessing from one-line comments.</mark>

## **Problem Hypothesis 3**

<mark>The</mark> **<mark>candidates at every experience level facing open-ended or behavioural questions</mark>** <mark>are most frustrated about</mark> **<mark>working out what the interviewer is really assessing</mark>** <mark>because they answer the first relevant thing that comes to mind, or go off on a tangent, and only realise the mismatch after the interview is over.</mark>
